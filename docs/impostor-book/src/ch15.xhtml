Chapter 15

Things to go and break

Reading a shader teaches you what it says. Breaking one teaches you what it does. These are ordered by how much you will learn per minute spent.

How to look at things

Two facts make experimenting cheap here. First, _apply_style runs every frame, so any RenderStyle field is live — edit the playground file, restart, and you see it. Second, the pipeline is context-agnostic, so you can render without a window at all:

import moderngl
from lammps_live.ui.gl3d import GLScene, view_matrix, proj_matrix

ctx = moderngl.create_standalone_context(require=330)
scene = GLScene(ctx, 820, 900)
scene.render(view, proj, centers, radii, directors, (11.9, 28.1))
img = scene.read_rgb()          # (900, 820, 3) uint8, row 0 = top

That validates the GLSL and the instance-buffer layout with no display, which makes it a fine place to try shader edits before wiring them into the app.

Level 1: turn things off

Every one of these is a single field in a playground's DEFAULT_STYLE.varied(...) call. Change it, look, change it back.

display_gamma = 1.0
Disables the linear-light conversion entirely (chapter 9). The whole image goes flat and chalky. This is the single most instructive one-character change in the file, because the failure looks like a lighting problem and is a units problem.
antialias = False
Chapter 11's pass, gone. Look at the sheet's far rows and then move the camera: the crawl is the thing FXAA was actually buying, more than the static jaggedness.
curvature_ao = False
One line of chapter 9, and beads immediately stop separating from each other. This is the cheapest effect in the renderer and one of the most valuable.
ao_strength = 1.0
Turns the exponent into a no-op. The occlusion is still computed, but nothing looks occluded — which tells you how much of "ambient occlusion looks right" is the raw term and how much is the curve applied to it.
outline_edge_fraction = 0.60
Well past sensible. Watch for the bright square in the middle of each bead that chapter 9 predicted from g = max(|dx|, |dy|). Seeing the prediction come true is worth more than reading the derivation twice.

Level 2: break an invariant deliberately

These need a shader edit in gl3d.py. Each targets one specific argument in the book.

1. The screen-parallel billboard (chapter 3)

In _GEOM_VS, replace the tangent-circle construction with the naive one:

vec3 corner = C + vec3(in_corner * r, 0.0);

Then look at the corners of the viewport, where chapter 3 computed that beads need a quad 70% larger than r. You should see beads with straight bites taken out of them along the frame edge, and the bite should grow as you move toward the corner. Now try the historical fix — multiply r by 1.15 — and confirm it helps in the middle distance and still fails in the corner.

2. Interpolated depth (chapter 4)

Comment out the two lines that write gl_FragDepth. The beads still look like spheres, individually. But watch two beads overlap: instead of intersecting along a curve, one will jump wholesale in front of the other. This is the artefact the entire hand-written-depth machinery exists to remove, and the module docstring's description ("z-jumps") is exact.

While you are there, measure the frame time with and without. The difference is early-z, and it is the price chapter 14 talks about.

3. The unmasked blur (chapter 8)

In _BLUR_FS, set float fg = 1.0; unconditionally. Every bead grows a bright halo, because the background's (1, 1) bleeds inward across the silhouette. Then work out why the halo is bright rather than dark — the answer is in chapter 7's very first lines.

4. Linear shadow steps (chapter 7)

Change shadowLen * f * f to shadowLen * f. The contact shadows — the dark seams where beads touch — largely vanish, because with 16 equal steps over 2.75 σ the first step is already a third of a bead radius out. Same sixteen lookups, badly spent.

5. Remove the jitter (chapter 6)

Set float jit = 0.5; in sun_visibility and float rot = 0.0; in the occlusion loop. The error does not get any larger, but it becomes structured: banding in the occlusion, terracing at shadow edges. This is the clearest possible demonstration that noise is not the enemy — pattern is.

6. Un-cancel the outline threshold (chapter 9)

In the composite, replace float t0 = outlineThresh * dist; with a constant, say float t0 = 0.15;. Now the outline is a fixed depth gradient rather than a fixed fraction of each bead's radius, so near beads get swallowed and far ones get nothing. Then orbit, and watch the outline width breathe as the beads change distance. That is the artefact the * dist and the focal_px derivation jointly remove.

Level 3: build something

7. Visualise the G-buffer

Write a debug composite that outputs one channel raw: frag = vec4(N * 0.5 + 0.5, 1.0) for normals, vec4(vec3(ao), 1.0) for occlusion, vec4(vec3(shadow), 1.0) for sun visibility, vec4(vec3(-P.z / 30.0), 1.0) for depth. Half an hour's work, and it is the single most useful debugging tool you can have in a deferred renderer — every bug becomes "which buffer is wrong?"

8. Separate the blur

Chapter 8's exercise: split the 4×4 masked box blur into a horizontal and a vertical pass, carrying the weighted sum and the weight sum through an intermediate texture. 8 taps instead of 16, at the cost of one more full-screen pass at 184,500 pixels. Then measure it, and be prepared for the answer to be "no difference" — at this resolution the pass setup may cost more than the taps.

9. Half-resolution depth of field

Chapter 14 identified DoF as the most expensive pass. Render it at half resolution into a smaller texture and composite it back, blending by circle of confusion so sharp regions keep full resolution. This is what production engines do, and getting the sharp/blurred boundary clean is the interesting part.

10. A second light

The composite has one sun. Add a second direction with its own colour and gain, and note what you can and cannot reuse: the diffuse and specular terms duplicate trivially, but the contact-shadow march in pass 2 would need a second 16-step loop and a third output channel. Working out the cost of that — and why real engines therefore use shadow maps once they have more than one or two lights — is the real exercise.

A last word

The thing worth taking from this renderer is not any single technique. It is the habit visible in every comment in gl3d.py and render_style.py: each number is expressed in units that do not change when something irrelevant changes. Occlusion radii in bead radii. Depth ramps in fractions of the scene's span. Blur in pixels at a reference viewport height. Outlines in fractions of a bead's on-screen radius, converted through the focal length so that both the distance and the radius cancel.

That discipline is why one style file drives a 7-bead patch, a 900-bead sheet and a 1500-bead assembly box, in a window or fullscreen, without any of them needing to be retuned. It is a much more transferable lesson than the ray-sphere formula — and unlike the formula, you will have to keep choosing it.
