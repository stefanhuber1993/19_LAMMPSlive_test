How to read this

You have ten years of Python and three days of shaders. This book assumes exactly that. Every piece of maths is written out and checked against a number, and every number is one your own renderer actually produces.

The scene the numbers come from is the MesoMem membrane sheet (lammps_live/playgrounds/mesomem_sheet.py): 900 beads of radius 0.5 σ, seen through the 820 × 900 pixel sim viewport, at the focal length the camera picks when it fits the sheet to that viewport. Those are the real values, pulled out of the running system, not round numbers invented for a textbook. One simplification: they are measured with periodic_images = (0, 0, 0), the single real cell, so that the depth span stays small and the arithmetic stays readable. Chapter 13 turns the images back on and gives the numbers for that.

The heart of it: the vertex shader that builds the quad and the fragment shader that ray-casts the sphere inside it. This is where the impostor technique actually lives.

A note on the maths notation

GLSL is written in code font. Vectors are bold in your head and plain on the page: N is a normal, P a position, L a direction toward the light. dot(a, b) is the dot product, which for two unit vectors is the cosine of the angle between them — that single fact is doing about half the work in this renderer, so if it is not automatic yet, make it automatic now.

The Python analogy that actually helps. A fragment shader is a function you write once and the GPU calls once per pixel, with no shared state between calls, no loops you control, and no way to look at the neighbour's answer. It is map() over about 700,000 items, run in parallel, where your function may only read from textures and write to its own output slot. Every strange thing in this book — why the blur needs a mask, why depth of field gathers instead of scatters, why antialiasing happens at the very end — follows from that one restriction.
