Chapter 2

Where a bead lives

Five coordinate systems, four transformations, and one division that does all the actual perspective. Get this chapter solid and the rest of the book is bookkeeping.

World space

Where the physics lives. LAMMPS reports positions in the simulation's own units — here reduced units, so distances are in σ, and the sheet's cell is about 23.6 σ wide. The membrane lies in the world xy-plane with its normal along +z; that is a convention this project chose, and the camera and the lighting are both built around it.

Nothing about the screen exists yet. A bead at (2, 0, 0) is two σ to the right of the origin, and that statement is true no matter where you put the camera.

View space: put the eye at the origin

The projection maths is much simpler if the camera is at the origin looking down its own axis. So the first transformation is a rigid motion — a rotation plus a translation, no scaling, no distortion — that carries the camera to the origin and drags the whole world along with it.

def view_matrix(eye, right, true_up, forward):
    R = np.array([right, true_up, -forward])
    t = -R @ np.asarray(eye)
    M = np.eye(4)
    M[:3, :3] = R
    M[:3, 3] = t
    return M

Read R row by row. The first row is the camera's right vector, so (R @ v)[0] = dot(right, v) — "how far along the camera's right is this?" The second row gives "how far up", and the third row is -forward, giving "how far behind the camera". That last minus sign is the OpenGL convention: the camera looks down −z, so things in front of you have negative view-space z.

Why negative z is worth tolerating. It is a genuine annoyance — you will write -P.z to get a distance about forty times in this codebase. But it makes the coordinate system right-handed with +x right and +y up, which matches every maths textbook you have ever read. The alternative (left-handed, +z forward) makes cross products come out backwards. Every GLSL sample you find online assumes the OpenGL convention, so take the minus sign.

The translation t = -R @ eye is just the rotation applied to the eye, negated: rotate first, then slide the rotated eye to the origin. If you prefer, view = R · (world − eye), written as one 4×4 matrix so it composes with the projection.

Worked example

The sheet's camera sits at eye = (0, −14.46, 14.18) looking at target = (0, 5.63, 0). A bead near the front of the sheet lands at view-space (2.0, 0, −12.0): two σ to the camera's right, level with the view axis, twelve σ in front of the lens. From here on we only ever use those three numbers — the world position has done its job.

The projection matrix, from a pinhole camera

Now the interesting part. Forget matrices for a second and think about a pinhole camera, which is what Camera3D literally implements:

sx = self.cx + cx * scale      # scale = focal / depth
sy = self.cy - cy * scale

A point cx units to the right, at depth d, lands focal × cx / d pixels right of the screen centre. That is similar triangles, and it is the whole of perspective: divide by the distance. Things twice as far away are half as big.

The GPU cannot literally divide inside a matrix multiply — matrices are linear and division is not. So it does something slightly sneaky: it keeps a fourth coordinate w, arranges for w to come out equal to the distance, and then divides by it in fixed-function hardware after the vertex shader returns. That post-shader division is called the perspective divide, and it is the only nonlinear step in the pipeline.

def proj_matrix(fx, fy, near, far):
    M = np.zeros((4, 4))
    M[0, 0] = fx
    M[1, 1] = fy
    M[2, 2] = -(far + near) / (far - near)
    M[2, 3] = -2.0 * far * near / (far - near)
    M[3, 2] = -1.0
    return M

The bottom row is the trick: M[3, 2] = -1 means clip.w = −z_view, which for a point in front of the camera is a positive distance. Everything else divides by that.

What fx and fy are

They are the pinhole focal length, rescaled so the screen runs from −1 to +1 instead of from 0 to W:

fx = camera.focal / (camera.viewport_w / 2.0)   # 444.4 / 410 = 1.0839
fy = camera.focal / (camera.viewport_h / 2.0)   # 444.4 / 450 = 0.9876

Check it. ndc.x = fx · x / (−z), and the viewport transform then maps −1..+1 onto 0..W, so the pixel offset from centre is ndc.x · W/2 = focal · x / (−z). The W/2 cancels the one in fx, and you are back at the pinhole formula. The projection matrix is a pinhole camera wearing a normalisation.

Note fx ≠ fy here because the viewport is 820×900 — taller than it is wide. The focal length in pixels is the same in both directions (a pixel is square); it is only the normalisation that differs. Get this backwards and your beads come out as eggs.

Clip space and the divide

Our bead at view (2, 0, −12). The frustum for this frame runs near = 10.411 to far = 29.577 — the renderer measures the bead extent and pads it, in _render_gl_3d:

near_clip = dmin - phys_r - 1.0     # 11.911 - 0.5 - 1 = 10.411
far_clip  = dmax + phys_r + 1.0     # 28.077 + 0.5 + 1 = 29.577

so M[2][2] = −2.0864 and M[2][3] = −32.1325. Multiply out:

clip.x = fx · 2      =    2.1678
clip.y = fy · 0      =    0
clip.z = −2.0864·(−12) + (−32.1325) · 1  =  −7.096
clip.w = −(−12)     =   12.0

Divide by w and you are in normalised device coordinates, the cube from −1 to +1 in all three axes:

ndc = (0.1807, 0.0, −0.5913)

and the viewport transform turns that into a pixel:

x_px = (0.1807 × 0.5 + 0.5) × 820 = 484.1
y_px = (0.0    × 0.5 + 0.5) × 900 = 450.0

which is exactly what Camera3D.project gives on the CPU: 410 + 2 × (444.4/12) = 484.07. The two paths agree, which is not an accident — gl3d.py's matrix helpers were written to match camera.py so the CPU overlays land on top of the GPU beads.

The depth mapping is not linear

The z component of NDC is what ends up in the depth buffer, remapped from −1..+1 to 0..1:

gl_FragDepth = 0.5 × (clip.z / clip.w) + 0.5   =  0.5 × (−0.5913) + 0.5  =  0.204

Our bead sits at 12 σ in a frustum spanning 10.4 to 29.6 — 8% of the way through — but it lands at 20% of the depth buffer. That is the 1/z in the divide: after dividing clip.z by w = distance, the mapping is hyperbolic, not linear.

This is why the near plane matters so much. Push near from 10.4 down to 0.05 and the curve becomes a cliff: essentially the whole 0..1 range is spent on the first fraction of a σ, and everything past 12 σ is crammed into the last few floating-point values — two beads a tenth of a σ apart get the same depth, and which one wins flickers frame to frame. That artefact is called z-fighting, and the reason you have never seen it in this renderer is the two lines above: the near plane is fitted to the scene every single frame.

Why widen the planes for the box outline. Just after those two lines, _render_gl_3d projects the eight corners of the periodic box and pushes near_clip/far_clip out to enclose them. The box outline is drawn as real GL lines through the same matrices (chapter 12), so a corner poking in front of the nearest bead would simply be cut off at the near plane. You would see an edge of the box vanish as you orbited — the kind of bug that takes an hour to attribute to the near plane.

The one function you will use constantly

Everything above runs forward: a 3D point becomes a pixel. Chapters 6 onward need the same thing in a hurry, inside a fragment shader, without a matrix multiply. That reduces to two lines, and they are worth memorising:

vec2 view_to_uv(vec3 p) {
    vec2 ndc = projScale * p.xy / max(-p.z, 1e-6);
    return ndc * 0.5 + 0.5;
}

projScale is just (fx, fy), uploaded as a vec2. That is the whole projection: multiply by fx, divide by the distance, shift into 0..1. Chapter 6 explains why this small function is the load-bearing wall of the entire post-processing chain.
