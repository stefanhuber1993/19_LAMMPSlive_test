Chapter 4

Ray-casting the sphere

Fifteen lines of fragment shader turn a flat quad into a sphere that is correct to the last pixel. This is the chapter where the illusion happens.

The setup

The fragment shader runs once for every pixel the quad covers — roughly 1100 of them for a front-row bead, 200 for a back-row one. Each invocation knows three things, all handed over by chapter 3:

  • v_rayView: this pixel's point on the quad, in view space. Since the eye is at the origin, normalising it gives the ray direction.
  • v_centerView: the sphere's centre, in view space.
  • v_radius: the sphere's radius.

And it must answer: does this ray hit that sphere, and if so, where?

Ray–sphere intersection

Write the ray as p(t) = t · dir with dir unit and t ≥ 0 the distance along it. A point is on the sphere when |p − c| = r. Square both sides to kill the square root:

|t·dir − c|² = r²
t²(dir·dir) − 2t(dir·c) + (c·c) = r²

and since dir is unit, dir·dir = 1, leaving a plain quadratic in t with a = 1:

t² − 2(dir·c) t + (c·c − r²) = 0

Write b = dir·c. The quadratic formula, with the 2s already cancelled:

t = b ± √(b² − (c·c − r²))

which is exactly the shader:

vec3 dir = normalize(v_rayView);
vec3 c = v_centerView;
float b = dot(dir, c);
float disc = b * b - (dot(c, c) - v_radius * v_radius);
if (disc < 0.0) discard;                  // ray misses the sphere
float t = b - sqrt(disc);                 // near intersection
if (t < 0.0) discard;
vec3 hit = dir * t;

Reading the three quantities geometrically

b = dot(dir, c)
How far along the ray you have to travel to be level with the centre — the point of closest approach. It is the projection of c onto the ray.
disc = b² − (c·c − r²)
Rearrange: disc = r² − (c·c − b²), and by Pythagoras c·c − b² is the squared perpendicular distance from the centre to the ray — the impact parameter squared. So disc = r² − impact²: positive when the ray passes closer than r, negative when it misses. √disc is the half-chord length.
t = b − √disc
Back up from the closest approach by half a chord to reach the front of the sphere. The other root, b + √disc, is where the ray exits out the back — correct, invisible, discarded.

Two things to notice. First, the hit distance ranges over 11.5 to 11.89 — a 0.39 σ spread, most of it in the last sliver near the rim. That variation is the sphere's bulge, and it is what will let one bead correctly poke through another. Second, disc collapses to zero at the silhouette, which means √disc has infinite slope there. The edge is mathematically sharp — and stays sharp in the output, which is chapter 11's problem.

discard, and what it really does

discard aborts the fragment. No colour is written, no depth is written, and — crucially — whatever was already in the framebuffer at that pixel is untouched. This is what makes the quad invisible: 27% of its area, plus everything outside the circle, simply never happened.

It is also brutally all-or-nothing. There is no "35% covered" — you cannot partially discard. Which is why every silhouette in this renderer is a hard staircase.

Writing the depth by hand

Here is the subtle bit that makes impostors actually work rather than merely look right in isolation.

The hardware, left alone, assigns each fragment the depth interpolated from the triangle's vertices. Our triangle is a flat quad, so every pixel of a bead would get roughly the same depth — the depth of the quad plane. Two beads whose quads overlap would then resolve by which quad is nearer, all-or- nothing, and you would get the exact pathology the module docstring describes: beads "jump" in front of and behind each other instead of intersecting.

So compute the real depth, from the real hit point:

vec4 clip = proj * vec4(hit, 1.0);
gl_FragDepth = 0.5 * (clip.z / clip.w) + 0.5;

Project the hit point through the same matrix, do the perspective divide by hand, and remap −1..+1 to 0..1. Chapter 2 worked exactly this arithmetic out: a hit at 11.5 σ in this frame's frustum lands at gl_FragDepth = 0.187; a hit at 11.89 lands at 0.204. The hardware depth test now compares two genuine sphere surfaces, and two overlapping beads intersect along a real circle — for free, with no sorting, in any order you feel like drawing them.

The price: early-z dies. Normally a GPU knows a fragment's depth before running the fragment shader, so it can test and reject occluded fragments without shading them. That is "early depth test", and it is a large win in a scene where beads overlap heavily. The moment a shader writes gl_FragDepth, the hardware can no longer know the depth in advance, so every fragment must be fully shaded before it can be rejected. You pay this on every impostor renderer ever written. It also has a knock-on effect in chapter 11: it is one of the reasons MSAA is off the table.

The normal, and everything downstream

vec3 N = normalize(hit - c);

The outward normal of a sphere at a point is the direction from the centre to that point. Since |hit − c| = r exactly, you could divide by r instead of normalising and save a square root; the code takes the robust option.

This N is the single most valuable thing the whole geometry pass produces. Chapter 9 uses dot(N, L) for diffuse light, dot(N, H) for the specular highlight, dot(N, V) for the fresnel rim, and N.z alone for a free curvature-occlusion term. Chapter 7 builds an entire hemisphere basis out of it.

Colouring the bead

MesoMem beads are not plain spheres — they have an orientation (the director n, the model's whole point), and the renderer paints that orientation onto the surface. The key is one dot product:

float s = dot(N, normalize(v_dirView));   // signed cos-latitude

s = +1 at the pole the director points toward, 0 all around the equator, −1 at the opposite pole. It is a latitude coordinate, computed with one instruction.

float cosl = abs(s);
float tt = clamp((cosl - (band_half - band_soft)) / (2.0 * band_soft), 0.0, 1.0);
albedo = mix(equator_col, pole_col, tt);

With band_half = 0.30 and band_soft = 0.07, the transition runs from cosl = 0.23 to cosl = 0.37. In degrees: yellow out to 13° either side of the equator, fully blue past 22°. That yellow band is the hydrophobic waist of the model lipid, and the blue caps are its hydrophilic ends — the physics is literally painted on.

Then a white cap over the +n pole:

float w = smoothstep(white_min - white_soft, white_min + white_soft, s);
albedo = mix(albedo, white_col, w);

white_min = 0.954 is cos(17.4°), so it is the top 17° of the bead. Note it keys off s, not abs(s) — which is the whole point: the yellow/blue banding is symmetric and cannot tell you which end is which, and this cap can. It survives into the energy colouring too, so switching to the inferno ramp costs you the energy scale but never the sense of the director.

The energy ramp

float t = clamp((v_energy - energyRange.x)
                / max(energyRange.y - energyRange.x, 1e-6), 0.0, 1.0);
float f = t * 31.0;
int i = int(floor(f));
albedo = mix(ramp[i], ramp[min(i + 1, 31)], f - float(i));

A hand-rolled 32-entry lookup with linear interpolation between neighbours — a numpy programmer will recognise np.interp. It is done in a uniform array rather than a 1D texture because 32 entries is small, it avoids a sampler binding, and the interpolation is explicit.

Notice the deliberate design decision recorded in the comment: this colour is flat across the bead, unlike the banding. The energy belongs to the whole particle, and shading it as a gradient would invite reading structure into a sphere that has none. That is a data-visualisation judgement expressed in three lines of shader.

The alpha channel, and the periodic clip

o_albedo = vec4(albedo * v_bright, v_fade);
o_normal = N;
o_viewpos = vec4(hit, 1.0);

Three outputs from one invocation — that is the G-buffer, and chapter 5 is about why. The alpha slot of the albedo would otherwise be wasted, so it carries v_fade, a per-bead strength that chapter 13's periodic images use to trail off into the background.

One more piece, for periodic scenes:

if (clipEnable > 0.5) {
    vec3 wp = (viewInv * vec4(hit, 1.0)).xyz;
    float dEdge = min(boxHalf.x - abs(wp.x), boxHalf.y - abs(wp.y));
    if (dEdge < 0.0) discard;             // outside the periodic cell -> gone
}

Transform the hit point back to world space and throw it away if it is outside the box. Note that this is a per-pixel clip, not a per-bead one: a bead straddling the boundary gets sliced exactly at the face, and its wrapped ghost on the opposite side gets the complementary slice, so the two halves add up to one whole bead. Any coarser test would make beads pop in and out at the seam.

And the discard matters for a reason worth internalising: a discarded fragment writes no depth, so it cannot falsely occlude something genuinely behind it. Had the shader instead drawn those pixels in the background colour, it would have punched a bead-shaped hole through everything behind them.
