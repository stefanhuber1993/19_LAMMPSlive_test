Chapter 9

The composite

One pass, one hundred lines, and the entire look of the renderer. Light, then edges, then distance, then the transfer curve that maps the physics onto a screen.

First: what space are these numbers in?

Before any lighting maths, one convention has to be nailed down, because getting it wrong makes everything downstream subtly wrong in a way that is very hard to diagnose.

A colour byte in theme.py — the bead's yellow, (247, 225, 122) — is a display value. It is not proportional to light. Displays are deliberately nonlinear: a pixel value of 128 emits roughly 22% of the light of a pixel value of 255, not 50%. That curve exists because human brightness perception is itself compressive, so encoding perceptually rather than physically uses the available bits far better.

Physics, though, is linear. Two lamps deliver twice the light of one. Every formula in this chapter — adding an ambient term to a sun term, multiplying by an occlusion factor — is only correct in linear light. So the renderer converts on the way in and back on the way out:

def to_linear(color255, gamma):
    return tuple(float(c / 255.0) ** gamma for c in color255)

At the very end of the pass, pow(col, vec3(invGamma)) encodes back to display space. In between, every number means light.

How this shows up if you get it wrong. The docstring records the symptom: display_gamma = 1.0 disables the conversion and gives "the flat, washed-out look this renderer had before". Skipping the conversion compresses the range — darks come up, colours desaturate, and shading gradients across a sphere flatten out. It looks like a lighting problem and it is a units problem.

The lighting model

vec3 N = normalize(texture(normalTex, uv).xyz);
vec3 L = normalize(lightDirView);
vec3 V = normalize(-P);                // eye is the origin in view space

Three unit vectors: the surface normal, the direction toward the light, and the direction toward the viewer. Note V = normalize(-P) — a one-line consequence of the camera being at the view-space origin, which is the whole reason chapter 2 put it there.

Diffuse

float diff = max(dot(N, L), 0.0);

Lambert's cosine law. A surface tilted away from the light spreads the same beam over a larger area, so the irradiance falls as cosθ, which for unit vectors is the dot product. The max(..., 0) clamps surfaces facing away, where the cosine would be negative — a "negative light" that would come out as a black smear.

Specular

vec3 H = normalize(L + V);
float spec = pow(max(dot(N, H), 0.0), specPower);

The Blinn–Phong highlight. H, the half vector, is the direction exactly between the light and the eye; a perfect mirror at this point would need its normal to be H to bounce the light into your eye. So dot(N, H) measures how close this surface is to being that mirror, and raising it to a power (spec_power = 32) makes the highlight tight — a bigger exponent is a smoother surface. The style file glosses the range usefully: "32 reads as plastic, 96 as a polished liquid".

Fresnel

float fres = pow(1.0 - max(dot(N, V), 0.0), 5.0);
col = mix(col, fresnelColor, fres * fresnelGain * ao);

Schlick's approximation. Every material becomes mirror-like at grazing angles — look along a sheet of paper and it shines. dot(N, V) is 1 facing you and 0 at the silhouette, so 1 − dot(N, V) is 0 in the middle and 1 at the rim, and the fifth power keeps it concentrated in the last sliver. The style comment names its effect exactly: this term "is most of what makes a bead read as wet rather than chalky".

Putting them together

col  = alb * (skyAmbient * ao + sunColor * diff * shadow * sunGain);
col += vec3(1.0) * spec * shadow * specGain;
col  = mix(col, fresnelColor, fres * fresnelGain * ao);

Two observations worth more than they look.

The specular is white, not alb-tinted. A highlight is light reflected off the surface before it enters the material, so it keeps the light's colour, not the pigment's. This is why a red plastic ball has a white highlight. Tinting it is one of the most common giveaways of a hand-rolled shading model.

Ambient occlusion multiplies the ambient term only. Look at where ao appears: on skyAmbient and on the fresnel, never on sunColor * diff. The comment is blunt about it — "AO darkens the AMBIENT term only — that is what it models. Scaling the sun by it too would just be a second, wrong shadow." Ambient occlusion answers "how much sky can this point see"; the sun already has its own visibility term, shadow, computed properly by marching. Applying AO to both double-counts and gives you mud.

Curvature occlusion, for free

if (curvAO > 0.5) ao *= 0.55 + 0.45 * smoothstep(0.0, 0.55, N.z);

A sphere occludes itself: near its rim, half the hemisphere above the surface is filled by the sphere's own body. And N.z — the normal's component toward the camera — goes to zero exactly at the silhouette. So this one line darkens each bead toward its rim by up to 45%, needs no samples at all, and does most of the work of visually separating overlapping beads.

The exponent

ao = pow(clamp(ao, 0.0, 1.0), aoStrength);     // aoStrength = 5.83

Why a power rather than a multiply? Because 1ⁿ = 1. Raising to a power leaves fully-open surfaces at exactly full brightness and bends everything below down — 0.9 becomes 0.57, 0.7 becomes 0.16, 0.5 becomes 0.018. Crevices plunge; open caps are untouched. A multiply, by contrast, would dim the whole image uniformly and you would have to raise the exposure to compensate. This is a good general trick for any 0..1 "visibility" factor you want to sharpen.

The outline

The most distinctive thing about this renderer's look, and a lovely piece of maths.

float dl = -texture(posTex, uv + vec2(-texel.x, 0.0)).z;
float dr = -texture(posTex, uv + vec2( texel.x, 0.0)).z;
float dd = -texture(posTex, uv + vec2(0.0, -texel.y)).z;
float du = -texture(posTex, uv + vec2(0.0,  texel.y)).z;
float g = max(abs(dl - dr), abs(dd - du));
float t0 = outlineThresh * dist;
float edge = smoothstep(t0, t0 * 4.0, g);
col = mix(col, outlineColor, clamp(edge * outlineStrength, 0.0, 1.0));

Four neighbouring depths, two central differences, take the larger. Where depth changes fast between adjacent pixels, ink. That is an edge detector — a Sobel filter's simpler cousin — run on depth instead of colour, which is why it finds geometric edges and ignores texture.

The threshold, derived

The question is what value of g should count as an edge. This is where the design gets careful, because the naive answer — pick a constant — falls apart immediately.

A sphere is curved, so its own surface has a depth gradient everywhere, and near the rim that gradient goes to infinity. Any fixed threshold therefore draws a ring at some radius on every bead. The question is: which radius? And the answer changes with how big the bead is on screen, which changes with distance, viewport size, and zoom. The comment records what that failure looks like:

"a threshold that draws a crisp 1px ring on a distant bead swallows a third of a near one (the ring is an axis-aligned band, so what is left of a swallowed bead is a bright SQUARE — unmistakable once seen)"

The square is worth pausing on, because it is a nice deduction. g is max of the x-gradient and the y-gradient, so the region where g stays below threshold is bounded by max(|x|, |y|) < const — a square, not a circle. Blow up the threshold and the surviving un-inked centre of the bead is square-cornered.

So the style file exposes the parameter in the units you actually think in — at what fraction of its radius should a bead start being outlined? outline_edge_fraction = 0.90 — and derives the shader's threshold. Here is the derivation.

Take a bead of world radius r at distance D, focal length f pixels. Two facts:

  1. One pixel spans D/f world units at that distance (chapter 2's pinhole formula, rearranged).
  2. At a distance s from the bead's centre in the screen plane, the sphere's surface stands √(r² − s²) in front of its centre plane, so its depth slope is ds/dz = s/√(r² − s²).

The central difference the shader computes spans two pixels, so:

g = 2 · (D/f) · s/√(r² − s²)

Substitute u = s/r — the fraction of the radius — and the r cancels top and bottom:

g = 2 · (D/f) · u/√(1 − u²)

Now set the shader's threshold to t0 = thresh × D (scaled by distance, which the shader does), demand g = t0 at the chosen u, and the D cancels too:

thresh = 2u / (√(1 − u²) · f)

Which is exactly the method in render_style.py:

def outline_threshold(self, focal_px):
    u = min(max(self.outline_edge_fraction, 0.0), 0.999)
    return 2.0 * u / ((1.0 - u * u) ** 0.5) / max(focal_px, 1.0)

Both D and r cancel. One number holds for every bead in the scene at every depth, and only the focal length — how big things are on screen — survives. Change the window size, go fullscreen, zoom: the camera reports a new focal_px and the outline stays the same width relative to the beads.

Push outline_edge_fraction to 0.94 and it is a hairline; drop it to 0.85 and it is heavy ink.

Depth cue

float c = clamp((dist - cueNear) / max(cueFar - cueNear, 1e-4), 0.0, 1.0);
col = mix(col, sky, c * cueStrength);

A straight linear fade toward the background colour with distance. Not physical fog — real atmospheric extinction is exponential — and the comment defends the choice: this is "a depth-perception aid from technical illustration", and it works better in a dense bead cloud precisely because the ramp is placed over the scene's own depth span rather than over some absolute physical scale.

def cue_range(self, d_front, d_back):
    span = max(d_back - d_front, 1e-4)
    return d_front + self.cue_start * span, d_front + self.cue_end * span

For the sheet (cue_start = 0.0, cue_end = 0.70) over 11.911 to 28.077 σ, that puts the ramp from 11.911 to 23.23 σ, reaching 60% of the way to the background there. Dolly the camera in and the ramp follows, because it is quoted as a fraction. Absolute distances would need retuning every time you moved.

Two fades and their order

if (strength < 1.0) col = mix(sky, col, clamp(strength, 0.0, 1.0));

if (edgeFade > 0.0) {
    vec2 dc = abs(uv - 0.5) * 2.0;         // 0 at center -> 1 at each edge
    col = mix(col, sky, edgeFade * smoothstep(0.55, 1.0, max(dc.x, dc.y)));
}

The first is chapter 13's periodic-image fade, arriving via the albedo's alpha channel. The second fades bead fragments toward the background near the frame edge, softening the clip seam on periodic scenes.

The comment on the ordering is a small lesson in composing effects: the image fade goes after the depth cue "because it is the same kind of statement — this is further away, let go of it — and before the screen vignette, which is about the frame rather than the scene." Effects that talk about the world before effects that talk about the picture.

Tonemapping

col = mix(col, aces(col * exposure), tonemapMix);
col = pow(clamp(col, 0.0, 1.0), vec3(invGamma));
vec2 q = uv - 0.5;
col *= 1.0 - vignette * dot(q, q);

Linear light is unbounded — a specular highlight can easily hit 3.0 — and a display tops out at 1.0. A tonemap is the curve that squeezes the one into the other. The naive answer, min(col, 1.0), turns every highlight into a flat white blob. Film does better: it rolls off gradually, so bright things stay bright but keep their shape. aces() is Krzysztof Narkowicz's famous rational fit to the Academy Color Encoding System's curve, five coefficients and one divide.

This renderer uses it at half strength, and the comment explains why:

"the full filmic S lifts the shadows and pulls saturated colour toward grey, which on a near-monochrome scene reads as washed out. Blending it half-and-half with the linear colour keeps the rolloff at half slope and gives the colour back."

That is a real trade honestly stated, including its cost: "the linear half is unbounded, so a very bright specular clips instead of rolling off — hence the clamp." Half of a filmic curve is not a filmic curve, and the code knows it.

Then the gamma encode, and the vignette. dot(q, q) with q = uv − 0.5 is 0 at the centre and 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5 in each corner, so vignette = 1.1 multiplies the corners by 1 − 0.55 = 0.45.

One structural detail

Look at where the tonemap block sits: outside the if (P.z < -1e-4) that separates beads from sky. Both go through it. The comment explains the bug that would otherwise appear:

"anything faded toward the background has to pass through the same transfer curve as the background, or a 'fully cued' bead lands several times brighter than the void it was supposed to disappear into."

The depth cue mixes a bead's colour toward sky in linear space. If the sky then got tonemapped and the bead did not, two values that were identical before the curve would differ after it, and a fully faded bead would show as a visible ghost against the background it was supposed to have become. Whenever you blend two things together, they must be in the same space and take the same path afterwards.
