Chapter 3

The billboard that is exactly right

The vertex shader's whole job is to place four corners. Doing it naively costs you the edges of your beads; doing it exactly costs four extra lines of algebra.

The naive quad, and why it fails

The obvious billboard: take the bead's centre in view space, build a square around it lying parallel to the screen, half-size r. In shader terms, corner = C + (in_corner.x, in_corner.y, 0) * r. It is one line, and it is what most first attempts look like.

It works perfectly for a bead in the middle of the screen and fails progressively as you move outward. Here is why.

The rays that graze the sphere form a cone with its axis pointing at the sphere, not down the view axis. When you cut that cone with a screen-parallel plane, you cut it at an angle θ, and an oblique slice through a cone is longer than a perpendicular one — by a factor of about 1/cos θ in the radial direction. The quad, sized r, is too short. The sphere's silhouette runs off the edge of its own quad, the fragment shader never gets those pixels, and the bead comes out with a straight bite taken out of it.

Here is the shortfall, computed exactly (the exact expression includes a correction for the sphere's finite angular size; the last row is the small-bead limit):

off-axis angle θ10°20°30°40°54°
a distant bead (r/d = 0.04)1.6%6.5%15.6%30.8%70%
a close bead (r/d = 0.2)3.8%9.2%19.5%37.3%—
the rule of thumb, 1/cos θ − 11.5%6.4%15.5%30.5%70%

Now put that against your own viewport. At f = 444.4 px on an 820×900 view, the half-width subtends atan(410/444.4) = 42.7° and the corner sits at atan(√(410²+450²)/444.4) = 53.9°. A bead in the corner of your screen needs a quad 70% larger than r. That is not a subtle artefact; that is a bead with a third of it missing.

The code comment records the previous fix — "the old fix was a blanket 1.15× enlargement" — and why it was unsatisfying: 15% is far too little in the corners and pure waste in the middle, where every extra fragment is a ray-sphere test that will end in discard.

The exact construction

The clean fix is to stop fighting the geometry. Build the quad in the plane of the true silhouette — perpendicular to the direction to the sphere — and the ellipse problem disappears, because the cone is circular about exactly that axis.

Consider a ray from the eye O that just grazes the sphere, touching it at T. Because it grazes, OT is tangent, so OT ⊥ TC and the triangle OTC has its right angle at T. Pythagoras gives the distance to the tangent point immediately:

t = √(d² − r²)

All such T form a circle around the axis OC. To place it, drop a perpendicular from T onto the axis. In a right triangle, the foot of the altitude from the right angle divides the hypotenuse into segments equal to the squares of the adjacent sides over the hypotenuse, and the altitude itself is the product of the legs over the hypotenuse. So:

circle centre, along the axis:   t² / d
circle radius:                   r · t / d

Which is line for line what the shader does:

float d = length(C);
vec3 fwd = C / d;
float t = sqrt(max(d * d - r * r, 1e-6));
float rc = r * t / d;              // silhouette circle radius
vec3 Cc = fwd * (t * t / d);       // silhouette circle centre

Why this covers the sphere exactly

Every ray from the eye that hits the sphere is inside the tangent cone. Every ray inside the cone passes through the disc bounded by the tangent circle. The quad, a square of half-size rc in that plane, contains that disc. So the quad's projection on screen contains the sphere's projection — at every angle, at every distance, with no fudge factor. And it is minimal, because the tangent circle is precisely the silhouette.

The square wastes 4/π − 1 = 27% of its area on corners that will discard. That is the price of using a quad at all; a hexagon would trim it to 10% and cost you two more vertices per bead plus a more complicated corner table. Nobody does it.

The numbers, for the sheet

A bead at d = 12 σ with r = 0.5 σ:

t  = √(144 − 0.25)  = 11.98958
rc = 0.5 × 11.98958 / 12  =  0.499566     (0.09% smaller than r)
Cc = 11.98958² / 12       = 11.97917      (0.021 σ nearer than the centre)

Both corrections are tiny, and that is the point worth taking away: the size correction almost never matters for small beads. What matters is the orientation — that the quad is perpendicular to fwd = C/d rather than to the view axis. That single change is what buys you the 70% in the corner of the frame. The rc and t²/d refinements are free once you are building the quad in that plane anyway, so you may as well be exact.

Building a basis out of thin air

You have a plane normal (fwd) and you need two perpendicular vectors to lay the corners out along. Any two will do — a square rotated about its own axis is still a square containing the same circle — so you just need some reproducible pair:

vec3 hint = abs(fwd.y) < 0.99 ? vec3(0.0, 1.0, 0.0) : vec3(1.0, 0.0, 0.0);
vec3 right = normalize(cross(hint, fwd));
vec3 up = cross(fwd, right);

The cross product of two vectors is perpendicular to both, so cross(hint, fwd) is perpendicular to fwd — exactly what we want — unless hint and fwd are parallel, in which case the cross product is the zero vector and normalize divides by zero. That is what the abs(fwd.y) < 0.99 test guards: if the bead happens to lie almost straight up in view space, swap to a different hint.

up = cross(fwd, right) needs no normalize, because fwd and right are already unit and perpendicular, so their cross product is automatically unit. One reciprocal square root saved per vertex, 3600 times a frame — not a lot, but it is free.

Does the branch cause a visible pop? No, and it is worth understanding why not, because the same trick recurs. When fwd.y crosses 0.99 the quad's rotation about its own axis jumps discontinuously. But the rotation is unobservable: the fragment shader tests each pixel against the sphere itself, and the sphere does not know how its quad was oriented. The discontinuity is in a quantity nothing downstream reads.

Handing the ray to the fragment shader

The last two lines are the ones that make the next chapter possible:

v_rayView = corner;
gl_Position = proj * vec4(corner, 1.0);

gl_Position is the required output — where this vertex lands. v_rayView is a varying: the GPU interpolates it linearly across the triangle, so each fragment receives the view-space position of its own point on the quad. And because the eye is at the origin in view space, that point is the ray direction, up to a scale factor. One normalize in the fragment shader and you have the ray from the eye through this exact pixel — without ever inverting a matrix.

That is a pattern worth naming, because it recurs whenever a fragment shader needs to know "where am I, in 3D?": put the answer in a varying at the vertices and let the interpolator do the work.

A wrinkle worth knowing about. Perspective-correct interpolation of a varying divides by w per fragment, which is correct for a point on the quad — and the quad is a flat plane in view space, so the interpolated v_rayView is genuinely a point on it. The ray is then exact. If you had instead interpolated a normalised direction, you would get the normalised-then-interpolated vector, which is not the same as interpolated-then-normalised, and the beads would deform slightly near the frame edge.
