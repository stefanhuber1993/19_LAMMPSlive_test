Chapter 13

Drawing an infinite membrane

A periodic cell is a window onto an unbounded system. Drawing one copy of it says otherwise. Twenty thousand instances and one fade function fix that, and everything downstream costs exactly what it did before.

The problem

The sheet is periodic in x and y: a bead leaving the right face re-enters at the left, and the physics is that of an endless membrane. Draw only the cell and the viewer sees a small square raft floating in a void — which is a false statement about the simulation.

The fix is to draw the neighbouring cells too. Because everything after the G-buffer is screen-space, this is unusually cheap: more instances make pass 1 more expensive and change nothing else in the pipeline. Occlusion, blur, composite, depth of field and antialiasing all still process 738,000 pixels.

The declaration

periodic_images=(2, (0.5, 3), 0),
image_fade_start=0.15,
image_fade_end=1.0,

Per axis, and each entry is either a single number (the same either side) or a (before, after) pair — and either may be fractional. So the sheet asks for two cells either side in x, and in y half a cell in front and three cells behind. The z count is 0 because the cell is not periodic in z, and the renderer clamps it there anyway: "asking for images along a free axis would be inventing matter."

The asymmetry in y is the point of the whole feature. It puts the real cell — the one carrying the controlled bead, its control net and the box outline — near the front of the block, with a little material in front of it and a long tail of copies receding to the horizon. You look into a membrane rather than at a raft.

Why it is declared, not derived. "Worked out per frame it would flip from one side to the other as the view swung past a right angle, and the whole block would jump." A camera-dependent choice of which side to extend would be discontinuous in camera angle. Anything discontinuous in a continuously varying input will eventually be seen to pop. Declaring it makes the block a property of the scene, not of the view.

Generating the copies

reach[axis] = (1.0 + 2.0 * before, 1.0 + 2.0 * after)
whole[axis] = [int(math.ceil(before)), int(math.ceil(after))]

Two different quantities, and the distinction is the whole implementation.

Work in half-widths, so the real cell spans −1 to +1 on each axis. n whole copies out from a boundary then reach to 1 + 2n. That formula accepts fractions happily — 0.5 gives a reach of 2.0, i.e. half a neighbouring cell — but you cannot generate half an instance. So: generate ceil(n) whole copies, then clip the resulting beads to the fractional reach.

offsets = [np.array([i, j, k]) * lengths
           for i in range(-whole[0][0], whole[0][1] + 1)
           for j in range(-whole[1][0], whole[1][1] + 1)
           for k in range(-whole[2][0], whole[2][1] + 1)]
P = np.concatenate([pts + o for o in offsets])

For the sheet: whole = [[2,2], [1,3], [0,0]], so 5 × 5 × 1 = 25 copies of 900 beads = 22,500. After the clip, 19,966 survive. All of it is vectorised numpy — a list comprehension over 25 offsets and one concatenate, then boolean indexing. No Python loop touches a bead.

Reach and side, per axis

signed = (P - centre) / half
side = (signed > 0.0).astype(int)
limit = np.take_along_axis(reach[None, :, :].repeat(len(P), 0),
                           side[:, :, None], axis=2)[:, :, 0]

signed is each bead's position in half-widths: inside the real cell it is between −1 and +1 on every axis. side picks which end of the (before, after) pair applies, because the two directions can reach different distances. The take_along_axis gymnastics is just a vectorised "for each bead and each axis, look up the reach on the side this bead is on".

The fade, and why it is a fraction

If the copies simply stopped, you would have swapped one false statement for another: instead of a small raft you would have a large one, with a hard straight edge announcing where the renderer gave up. So they fade to the background.

out = np.clip((np.abs(signed) - 1.0) / np.maximum(limit - 1.0, 1e-9), 0.0, None)
u = np.max(np.where(limit > 1.0, out, 0.0), axis=1)
span = max(style.image_fade_end - style.image_fade_start, 1e-6)
F = np.clip(1.0 - (u - style.image_fade_start) / span, 0.0, 1.0)

u is how far out this bead is as a fraction of how far the copies reach in that direction: 0 anywhere in the real cell, 1 at the outer edge of the block, and the furthest-out axis wins. That normalisation is the critical design choice, and the comment says why:

"Expressed as a fraction rather than in half-widths so the fade cannot be left promising range that is not drawn: at 1.0 it reaches the background exactly where the copies are cut, whatever the counts, and there is no straight edge to see. That is the one thing this has to get right — an abrupt boundary is worse than no images at all."

Work the sheet's asymmetry through. In x the reach is 5 half-widths either side; in y it is 2 in front and 7 behind. Quoted in half-widths, a single fade distance would finish long before the far y edge and long after the near one. Quoted as a fraction of each side's own reach, all four boundaries dissolve exactly where they are cut. With image_fade_start = 0.15, beads stay at full strength through the first 15% of the block and then fade linearly to nothing at the edge.

The bead that must be dropped, not faded

keep = (F > 0.01) & np.all(np.abs(signed) <= limit + 1e-9, axis=1)
# a fully faded copy is background-coloured but still writes depth,
# so it would punch a hole in whatever is behind it.

This is the sort of bug that is obvious in hindsight and baffling in practice. The fade is applied in the composite — the bead is drawn, it writes depth, and then its colour is mixed to the background. A bead at F = 0 is invisible and occluding: anything behind it vanishes, leaving a bead-shaped bite out of the scene with nothing visible causing it. So faded-out copies must be removed on the CPU, before they ever reach the G-buffer.

How the fade travels

Follow F through the pipeline, because it is a nice illustration of threading a per-instance value all the way to the end:

Python:    fades = F                          (per bead, 0..1)
VBO:       column 9 of the instance array
vertex:    in_fade -> v_fade                  (a varying)
fragment:  o_albedo = vec4(albedo * v_bright, v_fade)
G-buffer:  the albedo texture's ALPHA channel
composite: float strength = albedoSample.a;
           if (strength < 1.0) col = mix(sky, col, strength);

It rides in the albedo's alpha, a channel that was otherwise doing nothing — the beads are opaque, so alpha has no blending job. Free storage in a texture you already allocated is worth noticing when you need to get one more number from pass 1 to pass 4.

Two mutually exclusive strategies

The renderer has two ways of handling a periodic seam, and picks one:

if tiling:
    bpts, ... = self._periodic_image_instances(...)
    box_half, edge_fade_on = None, False
else:
    bpts, ... = self._wrap_ghost_instances(...)
    box_half = (self.box_x / 2.0, self.box_y / 2.0) if periodic else None
    edge_fade_on = periodic

Without tiling, a bead straddling a face is clipped to the box in the fragment shader (chapter 4) and a wrapped ghost is drawn on the opposite face carrying the complementary slice. With tiling, neither is needed, for a reason worth stating out loud:

"a bead poking out of the real cell is exactly the neighbouring copy's bead poking in, which is what the true infinite tiling looks like."

Drawing the neighbours correctly makes the seam handling redundant. That is the mark of the right abstraction: the special case disappears rather than being handled.

Twenty-two times the geometry, and the entire post-processing chain does not notice. That is the deferred architecture paying for itself.

The wider depth span does matter, though, and the renderer accounts for it. The frustum and the cue ramp are measured over what is actually drawn:

_, bdepth, _ = camera.project(bpts)
dmin = float(np.min(bdepth[bfinite]))
dmax = float(np.max(bdepth[bfinite]))

"With periodic images that is the difference between an infinite-looking membrane and one whose front and back rows are sliced off by the near and far planes — the images nearest the eye sit well in front of anything in the real cell."

The nearest instance is now at 3.5 σ rather than 11.9. Measure the frustum over the real cell only and the front two rows of copies get clipped away by the near plane — the exact rows that were supposed to make the membrane run off the bottom of the frame. Every scene-relative quantity in RenderStyle (the cue range, the focus plane) follows from this same pair of numbers, so they all move together.
