Chapter 11

Antialiasing after the fact

Every edge in this renderer is a cliff. The usual hardware fix is unavailable, so the last pass finds the cliffs in the finished image and smooths them by hand.

Three sources of hard edges

The module docstring names them, and each traces back to an earlier chapter:

  • The impostor silhouette. Chapter 4's if (disc < 0.0) discard; — a pixel is fully bead or fully nothing. There is no partial coverage.
  • The outline. Chapter 9's smoothstep over a depth gradient that, near the rim, diverges within a pixel — so the smoothstep saturates instantly and behaves like a step.
  • The periodic clip. Another flat discard, at the box faces.

Aliasing is what happens when a signal has detail finer than your sampling grid: an infinitely sharp edge has infinite frequency content, and sampling it at one point per pixel gives a staircase. Worse, when the geometry moves, the staircase crawls — individual pixels flip on and off as the edge slides across them. On the sheet, where hundreds of small beads each contribute two or three arcs of silhouette, this is the single most distracting artefact in an otherwise still image.

Why not MSAA

The standard hardware answer is multisample antialiasing: rasterise at four sample points per pixel, keep four depth values, shade once per covered primitive rather than once per sample, average at the end. It is nearly free in a forward renderer. Here it is not available, for two independent reasons the docstring names precisely:

Deferred rendering breaks the "shade once" trick. MSAA's efficiency comes from shading a pixel once and replicating the result to whichever samples the triangle covered. A deferred renderer does not shade during rasterisation at all — it shades in a later full-screen pass, which has no idea which samples belonged to which primitive. To make MSAA work you would have to keep the G-buffer at 4× resolution (that is 152 MB instead of 38) and run lighting, occlusion and outlining on every sample. That is not antialiasing; it is supersampling with extra steps.

gl_FragDepth makes it worse. Chapter 4's hand-written depth already forced early-z off. It also means the hardware cannot compute per-sample depth from the triangle's plane equation — the shader's depth value applies to the whole pixel — so the depth resolve within a pixel degrades exactly where the beads intersect, which is the one place you wanted it.

So: do it in screen space, on the finished image, as a post-process. That is FXAA — Timothy Lottes's fast approximate antialiasing, the technique that made deferred rendering practical for a generation of games. One full-screen pass, no extra geometry cost, no interaction with the G-buffer whatsoever.

How FXAA works

1. Luma

float luma(vec3 c) { return dot(c, vec3(0.299, 0.587, 0.114)); }

Rec. 601 luminance weights. Green dominates because human vision is most sensitive there; blue barely counts. FXAA works on brightness rather than colour because an edge you cannot see in brightness is one you barely see at all — the eye's spatial acuity for chroma is several times worse than for luma. Blurring such an edge would cost sharpness for nothing.

2. Find edges, and bail out fast

float lMin = min(lM, min(min(lNW, lNE), min(lSW, lSE)));
float lMax = max(lM, max(max(lNW, lNE), max(lSW, lSE)));
float range = lMax - lMin;
if (range < max(0.0625, lMax * 0.125)) { frag = vec4(rgbM, 1.0); return; }

Five samples — centre plus four diagonals — and their luma spread. Two thresholds combined with max: an absolute floor (1/16) so that near-black regions with tiny absolute differences are left alone, and a relative one (1/8 of the local maximum) so that bright regions need a proportionally bigger jump. Relative because perception is: a difference of 0.05 is obvious against a dark background and invisible against a bright one.

The comment identifies this line as more than a correctness measure — "which is most of the screen, so this early-out is also where the speed comes from". On a typical frame the great majority of pixels are smooth interiors or flat background, and they cost five texture fetches and nothing else.

3. Estimate the edge direction

vec2 dir = vec2(-((lNW + lNE) - (lSW + lSE)),
                 ((lNW + lSW) - (lNE + lSE)));

Two finite differences from the four diagonals: top pair minus bottom pair is the vertical luma gradient, left pair minus right pair the horizontal. The gradient points across the edge; rotating it by 90° — which is what the minus sign and the swap accomplish — gives the direction along it.

That distinction is the whole of FXAA. Blurring along an edge softens the staircase without losing the edge. Blurring across it would just be a blur.

float reduce = max((lNW + lNE + lSW + lSE) * 0.03125, 0.0078125);
float rcpMin = 1.0 / (min(abs(dir.x), abs(dir.y)) + reduce);
dir = clamp(dir * rcpMin, -8.0, 8.0) * texel;

Normalise by the smaller component so the step lands roughly one pixel along the shallow axis; reduce adds a floor proportional to local brightness, so that in dark areas — where the gradients are small and the direction estimate is mostly noise — the step shrinks instead of flailing. The clamp(…, -8, 8) caps how far a near-horizontal or near-vertical edge can reach.

4. Sample along the edge, and sanity-check

vec3 rgbA = 0.5 * (texture(srcTex, uv + dir * (1.0/3.0 - 0.5)).rgb +
                   texture(srcTex, uv + dir * (2.0/3.0 - 0.5)).rgb);
vec3 rgbB = rgbA * 0.5 + 0.25 * (texture(srcTex, uv - dir * 0.5).rgb +
                                 texture(srcTex, uv + dir * 0.5).rgb);
float lB = luma(rgbB);
frag = vec4((lB < lMin || lB > lMax) ? rgbA : rgbB, 1.0);

rgbA averages two taps close to the centre (at −1/6 and +1/6 of the step). rgbB adds two more out at the ends (−1/2 and +1/2), giving a wider average with more smoothing.

The last line is a safety net worth admiring. If the wide average's luma has escaped the neighbourhood's own range, the outer taps have walked off this edge onto something unrelated — a different bead, a bond line — and blending them in would smear two distinct objects together. So fall back to the narrow pair. It is a cheap, local test for "did my extrapolation leave the thing I was extrapolating?", and the same shape of check turns up in temporal antialiasing as neighbourhood clamping.

Why it must be last

The ordering is not arbitrary, and both constraints are stated in the comment:

After the tonemap and gamma encode. FXAA thresholds on luma against fixed constants like 0.0625, and those constants only mean anything in display space. An edge in linear light is not where the eye sees one — the gamma curve stretches the darks apart and squeezes the brights together, so a linear-space luma difference of 0.0625 is a wildly different perceptual step at the two ends of the range.

After the lines. The bond spokes, control net and box outline drawn in chapter 12 are one-pixel GL lines — about the most aliased thing you can draw. Running FXAA after them antialiases them for free.

So the final sequence is: composite → depth of field → lines → FXAA → final_fbo. Which is exactly what render() does, ending with:

self.shaded_tex.use(0)
self.final_fbo.use()
self._fs_vaos[self.fxaa_prog].render(mode=c.TRIANGLES, vertices=3)

What FXAA costs you

It is an approximation and it does not pretend otherwise. It has no idea where the geometry actually was; it infers edges from the image. So it will occasionally soften something that was meant to be sharp — a genuine one-pixel feature that looks like an aliased edge — and it cannot fix temporal shimmer, because it has no memory of the previous frame. The modern answer to both is TAA, which stores history and reprojects it, at the cost of ghosting and a great deal more complexity.

For a still-ish scene of round objects, FXAA is the right amount of machinery. style.antialias = False turns it off if you want to see what it was doing: the picture gets crisper and visibly jagged, most obviously on the sheet.
