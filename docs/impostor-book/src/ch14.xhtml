Chapter 14

The cost model

"1500 beads post-process for the same price as 7." That claim is true, and it is worth checking rather than believing, because where it stops being true is where you should look when the frame rate drops.

Counting the work

The viewport is 738,000 pixels; the half-resolution passes see 184,500. Here is roughly what each full-screen pass costs per pixel, counted from the shader source. Texture fetches dominate, so that is what is counted.

Two things jump out. Depth of field is by far the most expensive pass — 24 taps, each reading two textures, and it is at full resolution. Its early-outs are therefore load-bearing, and its worst case (a scene almost entirely out of focus) is several times its typical case. If you ever need frame time back, that is the first place to look: run it at half resolution like the occlusion pass, or cut the taps to 12.

Second, none of these numbers contain a bead count. That is the claim, and it holds exactly.

These are counts from reading the source, not measurements. Fetch counts are a decent proxy for the cost of a bandwidth-bound post-processing chain, but cache behaviour, warp divergence and the tiler on Apple Silicon all matter and none of them are visible from the source. Measure before you optimise; the point of the table is to tell you where to point the profiler.

Where the bead count actually shows up

Pass 1 is the only one that scales with the scene, and it does so in three places.

CPU: building the instance array

For the tiled sheet, _periodic_image_instances builds 19,966 rows × 10 floats and writes 799 kB to the GPU. All vectorised numpy, so it is a handful of milliseconds at most — but it is per frame, and it is Python. If you ever push to hundreds of thousands of instances, this is where the first wall is, and the fix is to keep the copies on the GPU (draw the base cell several times with an offset uniform) rather than materialising them in numpy.

Vertex shading

4 vertices × 19,966 instances = 79,864 vertex invocations, each doing about twenty floating-point operations. This is free. It was free at 900 beads and it is free at 20,000.

Fragment shading — the real cost

Here is where it bites, and it traces straight back to chapter 4.

Each bead's quad covers roughly (4/π) · πr² = 4r² pixels, where r is its on-screen radius. In the tiled sheet most copies are far away and small — call it 8 px average radius, so 256 fragments per instance, times 19,966 instances ≈ 5 million fragments on a 738,000-pixel screen. That is about 7× overdraw.

Normally a GPU would reject most of that with early-z: the front rows are drawn somewhere in the batch, and later fragments behind them would be killed before shading. But the impostor writes gl_FragDepth, so the hardware cannot know a fragment's depth until the shader has run. Every one of those 5 million fragments executes the full ray-sphere test, the banding, and the periodic clip — and then most of them are thrown away.

That is the true price of the impostor technique, and it is worth stating plainly because it is invisible in the source. It is still an enormous win over a mesh, which would have had the same overdraw plus a million triangles to set up. But it is where the tiled sheet spends its time, and it explains why the 7-bead patch and the 20,000-instance sheet do not, in fact, cost the same — the post-processing costs the same, which is the claim actually made.

Two numbers to keep in mind

The crossover. The post-processing chain does roughly 50 million texture fetches per frame regardless. The geometry pass does roughly 5 million fragment shader invocations for the tiled sheet, each substantially more expensive than a fetch. So the two are within a small factor of each other at about twenty thousand instances — which is a satisfying place to be, because it means neither half is obviously wasted. Below that, post-processing dominates and extra beads are nearly free. Above it, every additional bead costs.

The memory. 38 MB of framebuffers, fixed, plus under a megabyte of instance data. The framebuffers scale with the viewport, so going fullscreen at 2560×1440 roughly quadruples them to 150 MB — and quadruples every per-pixel cost in the table above. Fullscreen is the expensive mode, not the big scene.

The design principle, stated once

What makes all of this work is a single architectural decision made in chapter 5: convert the scene into a screen-sized data structure as early as possible, then never look at the scene again.

Every effect after that point is a function of the image, so its cost is a function of the image. The scene's complexity is absorbed once, in pass 1, and everything downstream is insulated from it. That is why adding ambient occlusion, contact shadows, outlines, depth cueing, depth of field and antialiasing to this renderer cost six full-screen passes rather than six traversals of the bead list.

The same principle is why a deferred renderer's costs are so predictable, and why its limitations (chapter 6: no off-screen occluders, no hidden occluders, guessed thicknesses) are exactly the things that are not in the image. The architecture buys you speed by forgetting, and what it forgets is what it cannot then do.
