Chapter 1

The problem with spheres

A sphere is the one shape a GPU cannot draw. It can only draw triangles. Everything that follows is a way of not paying for that.

What a mesh sphere costs

The obvious way to put a bead on screen is to build a triangle mesh that approximates a sphere and draw it. A UV sphere fine enough to look round at 19 pixels of radius wants roughly 32 segments around and 16 rings up, which is about 1000 triangles and 561 vertices. Multiply by the sheet's 900 beads:

900 beads × 1000 triangles  =  900,000 triangles
900 beads ×  561 vertices   =  505,000 vertex-shader invocations

And you would do that every frame, at 60 frames per second, for an object that covers — let us compute it — a circle of radius 18.7 px at the front of the sheet, so about 1100 pixels. You are running 561 vertex shaders to fill 1100 pixels. The mesh is more detailed than the screen it lands on.

Worse, the mesh is wrong. A 1000-triangle sphere has a visibly faceted silhouette when it fills a quarter of the screen, which is exactly what happens in the 7-bead patch scene. To fix that you refine the mesh, which makes the count worse, and you still have a polygon, not a sphere.

The impostor bargain

Here is the observation the whole technique rests on. A sphere, seen from any angle, has the silhouette of a circle. It has no interesting geometry at all from the outside — only its position, its radius, and the direction you are looking from. Everything that makes it look like a sphere rather than a flat disc is shading: the way the surface normal turns away from you toward the rim, and the way the surface bulges toward you in the middle so it can intersect its neighbours.

So: do not send a sphere. Send a flat quad — two triangles, four vertices — big enough to cover where the circle will land. Then, in the fragment shader, for each pixel the quad covers, work out whether the ray from the eye through that pixel would have hit the sphere. If it misses, discard the pixel and it is as if nothing was ever drawn there. If it hits, compute exactly where, and write out that point's normal and its depth as though a real sphere had been rasterised.

900 beads × 2 triangles  =    1,800 triangles
900 beads × 4 vertices   =    3,600 vertex-shader invocations

140× fewer vertex invocations, and the result is not an approximation of a sphere — it is analytically exact, correct to the last pixel, at every zoom level, forever. A quad that covers 4 pixels and a quad that covers the whole screen both give a mathematically perfect sphere. You cannot say that about any mesh.

That is an impostor: a piece of flat geometry standing in for a shape, with a fragment shader doing the actual work of being that shape. It is the standard technique in molecular visualisation for exactly this reason — VMD, PyMOL and every modern MD viewer draw atoms this way.

Instancing: the four corners, 900 times

You do not want 900 separate draw calls, one per bead — each one is a round-trip through the driver and they add up fast. Instead you use instanced rendering: upload the quad's four corners once, upload a per-bead array alongside it, and tell the GPU "draw that quad 900 times, and on the i-th time, feed the vertex shader row i of the per-bead array."

In gl3d.py the per-bead array is ten floats wide:

data = np.empty((n, 10), dtype="f4")
data[:, 0:3] = centers      # world position
data[:, 3]   = radii        # world radius
data[:, 4:7] = directors    # the bead's unit orientation vector
data[:, 7]   = brights      # albedo multiplier (the "spotlight")
data[:, 8]   = energies     # potential energy, for the inferno colouring
data[:, 9]   = fades        # 1 = solid, 0 = faded into the background

900 beads × 10 floats × 4 bytes = 36 kB, memcpy'd to the GPU each frame. That is nothing. And the declaration that binds it is one line:

self._geom_vao = self.ctx.vertex_array(
    self.geom_prog,
    [(self._quad_vbo, "2f", "in_corner"),
     (self._inst_vbo, "3f 1f 3f 1f 1f 1f /i", "in_center", "in_radius",
      "in_dir", "in_bright", "in_energy", "in_fade")],
)

The /i at the end of the second format string is the entire instancing mechanism. It means "advance this buffer once per instance, not once per vertex". Without it, the GPU would walk the bead array four times faster than intended and you would get garbage. With it, all four corners of bead i see the same centre, radius and director.

Where the CPU time actually goes. Notice that the buffer is only reallocated when the bead count grows (if n > self._inst_capacity). Steady state is one numpy fill and one write() per frame, with no GL object churn. This is the same instinct as preallocating an array outside a Python loop, and it matters more here, because allocating a GL buffer can stall the driver.

What you give up

Impostors are not free of consequences, and it is worth naming them now because three later chapters exist to work around them.

  • The fragment shader must write depth by hand. The quad is flat; the sphere is not. If you let the hardware interpolate the quad's depth, two overlapping beads resolve wrongly. Chapter 4 fixes this with gl_FragDepth — and doing so disables an important hardware optimisation, which is why chapter 11 cannot use MSAA.
  • The quad must be big enough. Off to the side of the screen a sphere projects to an ellipse, not a circle, and a naively sized quad slices the bead. Chapter 3 is entirely about getting this exactly right.
  • Every silhouette is a hard edge. discard is all-or-nothing: a pixel is either fully bead or fully background, with nothing in between. That is a jagged staircase, and chapter 11 has to clean it up in screen space.

All three are cheaper to fix than a mesh would have been. That is the bargain.
